    What the weather does to you

    Cold

    Your body loses heat faster than it makes it. First you shiver and your hands get clumsy; then hypothermia sets in: confusion, slurred speech, drowsiness, and the shivering stops. Older people, babies, and anyone wet, tired, hungry or who has been drinking alcohol cool fastest. Frostbite freezes exposed skin (nose, ears, cheeks, fingers, toes): it goes numb, then white or greyish-yellow and waxy. Wind chill tells you how cold it feels on bare skin. Wet clothing loses most of its warmth, so cold rain at 35–50 °F (2–10 °C) can be as dangerous as a dry freeze.

    Heat

    Your body cools itself by sweating. In heat it struggles: heat cramps, then heat exhaustion (heavy sweating, weakness, nausea, headache, dizziness, cool clammy skin), then heat stroke (body temperature over 103 °F / 39.4 °C, hot red skin, confusion, fainting). Heat stroke is an emergency: call 911 and cool the person with water and ice. The heat index is for shade; full sun can add up to 15 °F (8 °C). Night-time lows that stay warm give the body no chance to recover.

    Humidity

    Humid air stops sweat from evaporating, so it no longer cools you, which is why muggy days feel hotter than the thermometer says (the heat index). Very dry air does the opposite: sweat dries so fast you don't notice how much water you lose, and it dries out your skin, eyes, nose and throat, and can trigger nosebleeds and asthma. Dry cold air in winter does the same.

    Wind

    In cold weather wind strips away the warm layer of air next to your skin: that is wind chill, and it speeds frostbite and hypothermia. In mild heat a breeze helps by drying sweat. Above about 99 °F (37 °C) hot wind adds heat instead. Wind also dries you out. Gusts over 25 mph (40 km/h) make walking and holding an umbrella hard; over 40 mph (64 km/h) branches fall; over 58 mph (93 km/h) people can be knocked over and debris flies.

    Sunshine and UV

    Sunshine warms you: in full sun you take in much more heat than in shade, which is why heat stress (WBGT) is higher than the heat index on a sunny day. The sun's ultraviolet light burns skin and eyes and, over the years, causes skin cancer and cataracts. The UV index runs from 1 to 11+: at 3 and up use sunscreen (SPF 30+), a hat and sunglasses; at 8 and up unprotected fair skin can burn in under 20 minutes. UV is strongest from 10 am to 4 pm, and water, sand and snow reflect more onto you. Some sun lifts mood and makes vitamin D; long runs of grey days can lower energy and mood.

    Cloud cover

    Clouds block much of the sun's heat, so a cloudy summer day is easier on the body and a cloudy winter day feels colder. They block far less UV: thin or broken cloud lets most of it through, so you can burn on a hazy or partly cloudy day. Clear nights get colder, because the ground loses its heat to the sky; cloudy nights stay milder.

    Dew point (how muggy it feels)

    The dew point is how much water vapour is in the air, and it tells you how sticky it feels better than the relative humidity does. Below 50 °F (10 °C) the air feels dry; 50–59 °F (10–15 °C) comfortable; 60–64 °F (16–18 °C) sticky; 65–69 °F (18–21 °C) muggy; 70–74 °F (21–24 °C) oppressive, and 75 °F (24 °C) and up miserable, with sweat that hardly evaporates. High dew points also keep nights warm.

    Lightning

    Lightning kills and injures people every year, most of them outdoors doing something ordinary: boating, fishing, at the beach, on a sports field or doing yard work. It can strike 10 miles (16 km) from a storm, often before the rain arrives. When thunder roars, go indoors: a closed building or a hard-topped car. Porches, tents, picnic shelters and trees are not safe. Wait 30 minutes after the last thunder. On a boat, head in early; if caught out, stay low in the cabin away from metal. The forecast here uses the weather model's thunderstorm forecast and CAPE, the energy available to build storms (1,000 J/kg and up is enough for strong ones).

    Warm and cold nights

    Sleep is best in a cool room, about 60–67 °F (16–19 °C). Nights that stay above about 75 °F (24 °C) give the body no chance to recover from the day's heat; over several nights heat builds up, which is when most heat deaths happen, often older people alone at home without A/C. A fan helps up to about 90 °F (32 °C) but not above it. On freezing nights the dangers are indoors too: space heaters too close to bedding, and carbon monoxide from generators, grills or stoves run inside.

    Cold water

    Water takes heat from the body about 25 times faster than air at the same temperature. Falling into water below about 60 °F (15 °C) triggers cold shock: an involuntary gasp and fast breathing for about a minute, when you can easily inhale water. Then your arms and legs weaken within about 10 minutes, so swimming to shore often fails; hypothermia takes longer. A life jacket keeps you afloat through all of it.

    Air quality and pollen

    Smoke, haze and dust (fine particles) get deep into the lungs and bloodstream; ozone (smog), worst on hot sunny afternoons, irritates the airways. Both hit people with asthma, heart or lung disease, children and older adults first. Pollen does not harm most people, but for those with hay fever or allergic asthma it brings sneezing, itchy eyes and wheezing, worst on dry, warm, windy days; rain clears it from the air for a while.

    Visibility is the Secchi depth, worked out from Kd490 (how fast light fades in the water; lower is clearer). Satellites see the water a little offshore: the surf line, the bottom in shallow water and nearby land can throw readings off. Clarity is not water quality; check beach advisories for bacteria and red tide.
